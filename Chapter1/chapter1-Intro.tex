\chapter{Introduction}

Clinical documents form a large part of day-to-day healthcare operations. Referral letters, discharge summaries, laboratory reports, outpatient records, and clinical notes are routinely created during patient care and contain information needed for diagnosis, treatment planning, follow-up decisions, and administrative activities. Although these documents are rich in clinical information, much of the content exists as unstructured text, making large-scale analysis and automated processing difficult.

During discussions with healthcare practitioners and through examination of NHS clinical records, it became apparent that a significant amount of useful information remains embedded within narrative documents rather than being available in a structured format. Retrieving specific clinical details often requires manual review, particularly when information is spread across multiple documents or presented in different layouts.

The widespread adoption of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) has increased the availability of digital clinical data, but it has not eliminated the challenges associated with processing clinical documents. Healthcare organizations continue to manage documents originating from different systems, specialties, and workflows. With more documents coming in, manual extraction and coding take way more time, adding extra pressure on clinical and admin staff. 

In recent years, recent improvements in Natural Language Processing (NLP), particularly in transformer language models, and in OCR have enabled to process clinical documents automatically in practice. Modern language models have proven to automatically recognize clinically significant concepts from unstructured text, and OCR technology can make scanned documents and images of clinical documents intelligible to the computer. Both of these approaches can help us automate some clinical information extraction processes.

In this project, we examine how both these technologies could be implemented together in a single system. The outcome of this project is an Intelligent Clinical Document Processing system (ICDPS), capable of processing PDF and image-based clinical documents through a single processing system, combining OCR, document understanding, clinical concept extraction, and mapping to SNOMED CT. Our goal is to produce the unstructured clinical information in a format appropriate for use in clinical coding, document review, and health information management activities.

This chapter introduces the problem domain, outlines the motivation for the work, defines the project objectives and scope, reviews relevant literature, identifies research gaps, and presents the methodology adopted during system development.

\section[Introduction]
{\textbf{Introduction}}

The processing of clinical documents remains a practical problem in many healthcare settings. Documents such as referral letters, discharge summaries, reports and prescriptions are generated routinely and provide useful data for patient care and administrative purposes. Although much clinical documentation is being moved into electronic format, a significant proportion of the contents are unstructured and so inaccessible for automated processing.

A study of clinical documents within the NHS encountered during the work for this project showed the varied nature of the available documentation. The texts could be extracted as selectable digital information, while other documents were scanned and were therefore only available as PDFs or image files. The format of these documents also varied significantly across different origins of documents which made template-based extraction unreliable.

Clinical coding and information extraction are still frequently achieved using a manual approach. Although human review can interpret medical terminology with greater ease than a computer, it consumes time and resources, especially when dealing with vast numbers of documents, and can still vary from user to user. However, transformer-based language models are proving effective in clinical NLP applications, and several research papers suggest their accuracy for tasks such as medical entity extraction using documents such as BioBERT and ClinicalBERT. OCR accuracy is also improving, increasing the potential to access scanned clinical records.

This project aims to develop a framework independent of the structure of clinical documents, capable of producing coded output from a number of varied formats using an OCR, named entity recognition, classification and terminology mapping approach.

\subsection[Terminology and Definitions]
{\textbf{Terminology and Definitions}}

The development of the Intelligent Clinical Document Processing
System (ICDPS) involves several concepts and technical terms
from healthcare informatics, Natural Language Processing (NLP),
Artificial Intelligence (AI), and clinical coding systems.
Understanding these terms is essential for interpreting the
design and implementation aspects of the proposed system.
The important terminology used throughout this report is
presented below.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Clinical Document:}
A healthcare-related document containing patient information,
medical history, diagnoses, prescriptions, laboratory reports,
referrals, discharge summaries, and clinical observations.

\item \textbf{Natural Language Processing (NLP):}
A branch of Artificial Intelligence concerned with enabling
computers to understand, process, analyze, and generate human
language.

\item \textbf{Optical Character Recognition (OCR):}
A technology used for converting text embedded within scanned
documents or images into machine-readable textual information.

\item \textbf{Named Entity Recognition (NER):}
A Natural Language Processing task used to identify and classify
specific entities from text into predefined categories such as
diseases, medications, procedures, symptoms, and patient details.

\item \textbf{SNOMED CT:}
Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine -- Clinical Terms,
a comprehensive clinical terminology system used to represent
medical concepts in a standardized manner.

\item \textbf{Electronic Health Record (EHR):}
A digital version of a patient's medical history that includes
clinical data collected and maintained across healthcare systems.

\item \textbf{Transformer Model:}
A deep learning architecture that uses attention mechanisms to
capture contextual relationships within sequential data and is
widely used in modern NLP systems.

\item \textbf{Intelligent Clinical Document Processing System (ICDPS):}
The proposed framework designed to automate extraction of
clinical information from heterogeneous healthcare documents
and map extracted information to standardized SNOMED CT codes.

\end{itemize}

\section{Overview of the Project Topic}

Clinical documents contain a large amount of information that is essential for patient care, including diagnoses, medications, laboratory results, procedures, and clinical observations. Although this information is routinely recorded within healthcare systems, much of it remains embedded in unstructured text documents. Extracting clinically meaningful information from these documents is a challenging task that requires techniques from healthcare informatics, natural language processing, and machine learning.

The advancement of recent NLP techniques has enabled automatic recognition of medical terms, classification of clinical data, and aided standardization of clinical coding automatically. This has opened new possibilities for automated processing of clinical documents; however, practical problems related to extracting, storing and standardizing information from diverse healthcare records exist. Sections below analyze the overall problem and describe factors contributing to the design of the current system.

%=========================================================
\subsection{Global Scenario}

Clinical information is being generated and recorded digitally more than ever before within healthcare systems around the world. Referral letters, discharge summaries, outpatient notes, clinical notes, lab results are being regularly created and recorded digitally, leading to the accumulation of vast amounts of clinical documentation that must be read, interpreted, and managed.

This increase in digital clinical documentation has fueled interest in the fields of natural language processing and automated information extraction for clinical purposes. Initial clinical NLP tools were often based on handmade rules, dictionaries of keywords, or predefined document templates. These methods were well-suited to clinical documentation that adhered strictly to the system's implicit assumptions about its format, terminology, and content. However, performance deteriorated when applied to clinical documents that diverged significantly from these expectations.

Current research in this area is now focusing on the use of transformer-based language models that can learn context from clinical documents. Models such as BioBERT~\cite{ref1} and ClinicalBERT~\cite{ref2} have demonstrated high accuracy in performing tasks such as clinical named entity recognition, concept extraction and general biomedical text processing, making the automated processing of large volumes of clinical documentation more feasible than in the past.

In conjunction with developments in the field of NLP, there has been increased attention from healthcare organizations towards standardization and interoperability of clinical terminology. SNOMED CT is one standard for representing medical concepts across multiple healthcare organizations. There is an increasing need to identify information in unstructured clinical documents and transform it into standardized representations.

%=========================================================
\subsection{Societal Relevance}

The impact of clinical document processing extends beyond technical efficiency and affects multiple groups involved in healthcare delivery.

For patients, important clinical information is often distributed across referral letters, discharge summaries, investigation reports, and consultation records. Ensuring that relevant information can be identified and recorded consistently helps reduce the risk of incomplete documentation and improves the availability of information during future clinical encounters. Structured clinical records also simplify information sharing when patients receive care from multiple healthcare providers.

For healthcare professionals, reviewing large numbers of clinical documents is a routine but time-consuming activity. Information extraction and clinical coding frequently require manual examination of lengthy documents before relevant findings can be recorded. Automating repetitive extraction tasks can reduce administrative effort and allow clinicians and coding staff to spend more time on activities that require clinical expertise and decision-making.

Healthcare organizations also rely heavily on structured clinical information. Coded data is used for service reporting, audit activities, resource allocation, quality improvement programmes, and clinical research. Consistent use of standardized terminologies such as SNOMED CT improves interoperability between healthcare systems and reduces ambiguity when information is exchanged between organizations. As healthcare records continue to grow in volume, tools that assist with extracting and standardizing information are becoming increasingly relevant to routine healthcare operations.

\subsection{Problem Area}

Through developing this project, it was quickly noted that there are numerous difficulties encountered when processing clinical documents that standard information extraction methods do not address easily. Although huge steps have been made in clinical NLP, most practical clinical documents are not automatically processed easily, as there are differences between these documents in format, the structure of their content, and use of language.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Difference in documents:} An assortment of documents were taken into account for the scope of this project; referrals letters, discharge summary, lab report, scanned mail and images. It was clear that there was little consistency between them and that relying solely on templates to determine where information should be present is not feasible in such a scenario.

    \item \textbf{Difference in language:} Language is inherently ambiguous and there is often a need for context in understanding clinical concepts. Same clinical concept may be presented by a synonym or an abbreviation/short hand for the term.

    \item \textbf{Different forms of inputs:} Majority of existing systems work on digital text and ignore the reality of most clinical organizations where documents, for example referral letters or archives are in the form of image, and require an OCR process to be performed first. This may add errors due to inaccurate OCR, or variations in quality.
\end{enumerate}

The problems mentioned above make it clear that information extraction from clinical documents cannot be treated as an isolated task from the need to understanding text, and using terminologies to codify it. Hence the system, ICDPS.

\section{Specific Details of the Project Topic}

ICDPS was created to process diverse clinical document formats and convert the information contained within into meaningful, structured, clinical data. Each phase of processing contributes to an overall processing pipeline; each phase receives an output from its predecessor component to perform its assigned task.

The first phase is document ingestion. Clinical documents come in many different formats: PDFs generated electronically, scanned reports, referral letters, or imaging records. Early on, it was found that there was no single way to parse and extract data. For documents where text is readily accessible, they are parsed immediately; however, scanned or imaging documents are sent to an OCR (Optical Character Recognition) program and the output becomes available for further processing. At this point, all documents exist as uniform, normalized text.

The second phase is clinical information extraction. This phase uses information from the document text, specifically identifies medically related entities such as diseases, drugs, medical procedures, anatomical locations, allergies, laboratory values, or time indicators. This stage is vital to the overall function of the system because downstream tasks such as summary generation and SNOMED CT coding are entirely dependent on the extracted clinical information.

Once the medical entities are extracted from the clinical documents, they are organized and grouped together to aid in document interpretation and review. Grouping related concepts helps to present the crucial information in an organized manner that differs from the free text document presented originally. This phase was added to the design to help to decrease the amount of time needed to find relevant information within lengthy documents.

Summarization of the document text is also generated. Instead of relying on a user to navigate an entire clinical document to find important data, a condensed version, highlighting the most important clinical information identified in extraction, is created. This phase helps to speed up review of extensive medical records.

One last functionality that was added to the ICDPS is that of standardized clinical coding. After extraction, the clinical data goes through a SNOMED CT mapping module. Using semantic similarity searches, potential mappings are presented to a healthcare professional who reviews the options based on contextual information from the document and chooses the most appropriate code for the information.

\section{Purpose and Motivation}

This project was initiated due to practical issues faced in processing large volumes of clinical documents. The information in referral letters, discharge summaries, investigation reports and other clinical documents is crucial in patient care but most of it is still locked in unstructured text form. Manual inspection is frequently necessary to retrieve data of interest, especially given the varied nature, size and presentation of clinical documents.

It was clear from the preliminary investigation of current processes that much of document review and clinical coding relies on monotonous data extraction, i.e., clinicians repeatedly need to locate a diagnosis, drug, procedure or other clinical findings prior to assigning these details or encoding them. The present system was hence developed to aide in these processes, namely by attempting to locate the desired clinically relevant pieces of information within documents, and extracting the information, based on text within the documents.

Another goal was to investigate how recent clinical NLP techniques could be adapted and integrated into a complete end-to-end document processing pipeline. The objective was not to replace clinical expertise, but to aid the clinician by structuring and encoding required information, to facilitate quicker document review and to eliminate routine documentation effort.

The challenge of terminology standardization also provided a motivation for this project. AssigningSNOMED CTcodes to clinical descriptions represented in natural language is not always trivial, and several SNOMED CTconcepts might be relevant to a given clinical observation, depending on the context. The suggestions of SNOMED CTcodes were given in a confidence-ranked manner to provide the user assistance in choosing the appropriate concept and the final code decision rests with the user.

From a technical stand point, the advent of Biomedical language models and availability of clinical NLP datasets and established evaluation benchmarks prompted this project to research modern document-processing technologies in an academic setting. With the existing technologies, it was possible to design and implement an end-to-end clinical document-processing pipeline for data ingestion, information extraction, summarization and SNOMED CTcoding.

\section{Problem Statement}

Clinical information is conventionally contained within referral letters, discharge summaries, laboratory reports and various other narrative forms of healthcare records. These documents capture much of the necessary clinical, coding and record-keeping information but the data is locked away within free text, typically necessitating manual searching and extraction, particularly where different documents display unique structures and formats.

An investigation into a number of sample NHS clinical documents pertinent to this project revealed several real-world challenges; documents had greatly varied structures, scans and image-based records needed processing via OCR before any form of content analysis could take place, and the language used within many reports incorporated a multitude of clinical concepts that may have been expressed via the use of abbreviations, synonyms or terminology specific to the document and context in question. The above issues severely limited the efficacy of template or document-specific rule or keyword-based document processing techniques.

This project seeks to address the issue of creating a template-independent system to process varying and often dissimilar PDF and image based clinical documents, that aims to extract clinically significant information, provide summaries in a structured manner and generate SNOMED CT codes with their confidence values.

\section{Objectives}

The objectives of the project are:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item To build a framework to ingest documents, handling both scanned PDFs and digital PDFs and choosing either direct text extraction or image-based Optical Character Recognition (OCR) based on the properties of the document.

    \item To build a BioBERT based clinical entity extraction system which is able to extract and categorize different kinds of clinical entities such as diagnosis, medication, procedure, allergies, lab values and anatomical locations from free text clinical documents.

    \item To create a structured document summary of a clinical document and create role-based review categories for all extracted information.

    \item To build a SNOMED CT mapping system, capable of candidate term retrieving and candidate scoring with the help of techniques such as semantic similarity and relevance score using context.

    \item To perform terminology mapping with high accuracy by providing confidence-ranked SNOMED CT code suggestions to extracted clinical entities.

    \item To perform an end-to-end evaluation on representative clinical documents and NLP metrics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Relevance}

This research will look at the design, implementation and evaluation of an Intelligent Clinical Document Processing system (ICDPS) that can process English documents that are clinical in nature and obtain structured data from it. This system will take information from different document processing technologies, clinical NLP and term mapping technologies, combine them, and translate the clinical text into a structured format that can be utilized in clinical applications downstream.

This system takes various documents including digital PDF documents, scanned images documents as well as images of documents in formats such as JPEG, PNG, TIFF images. Text is extracted using either direct PDF parsing or OCR, depending on the document type. The extracted content is then processed through a clinical NLP pipeline that performs entity extraction, information categorization, summarization, and SNOMED CT mapping. The final output is generated in a structured machine-readable format, allowing extracted information and coding suggestions to be reviewed by end users before acceptance.

The scope of the project is limited to clinical document processing, information extraction, summarization, and SNOMED CT terminology mapping. The system was not designed to perform medical diagnosis, recommend treatments, or provide clinical decision support. Integration with live Electronic Health Record systems, real-time patient monitoring platforms, and large-scale healthcare infrastructure was not included within the implementation developed for this project. Similarly, support for non-English clinical documents and highly complex handwritten records was not investigated.

Development and evaluation were conducted within a controlled experimental environment. The implemented workflow processes documents in batch mode, allowing individual pipeline components to be developed, tested, and evaluated independently. Processing in real-time services, large scale deployment into an enterprise system and interface into real-time operational clinical systems are future work.

Another assumption made by the system is that the input documents are clean and fit for OCR and subsequent NLP processing. During experimentation, document quality has proven to be a significant factor in extraction accuracy, specifically in scanned clinical documents which have an image quality that include image noise, document skew, low contrast and strange layouts. Hence the overall accuracy of the system is related to the quality of the document and the accuracy of the processing steps prior to document quality.

Despite the shortcomings listed above the work clearly highlights the integration ofOCR, clinical information extraction, document summarization, semantic information retrieval and SNOMED CT mapping within a single framework. The system provides a working proof-of-concept demonstrating how unstructured clinical documents can be converted into structured data suitable for review and coding.

\section[Literature Review]{\textbf{Literature Review}}

A comprehensive review of the existing literature on clinical document processing, biomedical NLP, information extraction, and clinical terminology mapping was performed before system design. The main aim was to find appropriate approaches to handle heterogeneous clinical documents, as well as to observe the limits and open issues in current work.

A special emphasis was put on the existing works regarding named entity recognition in clinical text, transformer-based models in the biomedical domain, OCR based document processing, retrieval based on content and meaning, ontology mapping and medical information systems. These research areas represent the most important technological aspects of the presented ICDPS system and guided the selection of techniques and methods in the system implementation.

In the review, several common and repeated issues encountered in many works on clinical document processing were identified: structure diversity in documents, dependence on domain specific annotations, semantic ambiguity and processing limitations of scanned and image based clinical documents. Knowledge about these problems was essential in setting the problem statement and the project aims.

\subsection[Literature Survey]{\textbf{Literature Survey}}

Table~\ref{tab:lit_survey} summarizes representative studies relevant to the proposed work. The reviewed literature spans several research areas, including biomedical language models, clinical entity extraction, OCR-assisted document processing, semantic retrieval, ontology mapping, relation extraction, de-identification, explainable artificial intelligence, and healthcare information systems.

Each study was examined in terms of its methodology, reported outcomes, strengths, limitations, and relevance to the objectives of this project. The review was conducted to identify techniques applicable to different stages of the clinical document processing pipeline and to understand how existing approaches address challenges associated with information extraction, document understanding, and terminology standardization.

A number of recurring trends were observed across the literature. While many studies demonstrated strong performance on individual tasks such as named entity recognition, document classification, or concept retrieval, comparatively fewer works addressed the complete workflow from document acquisition to standardized clinical coding. In addition, support for heterogeneous document formats, integration of OCR with downstream NLP components, and accurate mapping of extracted entities to clinical terminologies remained areas requiring further investigation.

These observations influenced a number of design decisions adopted in the proposed system, including the use of transformer-based language models for information extraction, OCR support for image-based documents, and semantic retrieval techniques for SNOMED CT concept mapping.

\subsection[Research Gap Identification]{\textbf{Research Gap Identification}}

Based on the literature review, the following research gaps were identified that directly motivated the design of the proposed ICDPS:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Template Dependency:} The majority of deployed clinical extraction systems rely on fixed document templates or keyword lists. No existing open-source system provides a robust template-free extraction engine validated on heterogeneous NHS-format clinical documents.
    
    \item \textbf{Limited Image-Based Document Support:} Most transformer-based clinical NLP pipelines operate exclusively on digitally native text. There is a gap in end-to-end systems that seamlessly integrate OCR for image-based documents with deep learning-based extraction.
    
    \item \textbf{Confidence-Ranked SNOMED CT Suggestion:} While several tools provide SNOMED CT lookup functionality, very few provide contextually ranked code suggestions with calibrated confidence scores suitable for integration into clinical review workflows.
    
    \item \textbf{Multi-Role Clinical Categorization:} Existing systems extract and present all clinical entities in a flat list without differentiating between entities requiring action by different clinical roles (Pharmacist vs. Clinician). This omission reduces the practical utility of extraction outputs in multi-role clinical environments.
    
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility and Open-Source Availability:} Many high-performing clinical NLP systems described in the literature are not publicly available, limiting reproducibility and practical deployment. The proposed system is designed with open-source tools and reproducible experimental protocols.
\end{itemize}
\section{Methodology and Architectural Roadmap}

The project followed an iterative SDLC comprising five phases: requirements analysis, system design, implementation, testing, and deployment preparation. Each phase produced defined deliverables reviewed against the project objectives.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 --- Requirements Analysis:} Clinical workflow requirements were elicited through a review of NHS clinical coding guidelines and analysis of representative document samples. Functional and non-functional requirements were documented in the Software Requirements Specification (SRS) presented in Chapter 3.

    \item \textbf{Phase 2 --- System Design:} The modular pipeline architecture was designed, specifying the interfaces between the document ingestion, extraction, summarization, and SNOMED CT mapping modules. High-level and detailed design documentation are presented in Chapters 4 and 5 respectively.

    \item \textbf{Phase 3 --- Implementation:} Each module was implemented and integrated in Python 3.10 using the Hugging Face Transformers library, PyMuPDF, Tesseract OCR, and FAISS for vector similarity search. The interactive review interface was implemented as a Flask web application.

    \item \textbf{Phase 4 --- Testing:} The system was evaluated on a curated test corpus comprising 150 de-identified clinical documents. Extraction performance was measured using token-level F1-score, precision, and recall. SNOMED CT mapping performance was evaluated using precision@1 and precision@3 metrics.

    \item \textbf{Phase 5 --- Deployment Preparation:} System documentation, deployment scripts, and NHS integration guides were produced. The system was containerized using Docker for repeatable deployment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Expected Outcomes}

The project delivers the following outcomes:

\begin{itemize}

\item Anl end-to-end ICDPS pipeline that processes PDFs and image-based clinical documents.

\item A fine-tuned BioBERT NER model achieving F1-score $\geq$ 0.88 on clinical entity extraction across all eight entity categories.

\item A SNOMED CT mapping module achieving mapping precision@1 $\geq$ 0.85 on the test corpus.

\item A web-based clinical review interface supporting multi-role categorization and code acceptance/rejection workflows.

\item Technical documentation, system integration guide, and a Docker deployment package.

\item A comparative evaluation report benchmarking the ICDPS against cTAKES and a rule-based baseline system.

\end{itemize}
